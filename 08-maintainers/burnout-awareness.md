# Burnout Awareness

Maintainer burnout is real and common. Recognizing it in others helps
you interact better. Recognizing it in yourself helps you avoid it.

## Signs in a Maintainer

### Communication shifts

- Replies get shorter over time.
- Tone shifts from warm to neutral to cold.
- Long silences become normal.
- Stale-bot deployed aggressively.

### Engagement patterns

- PRs sit longer.
- Issues triage stops.
- Bug reports get terse "won't fix."
- Release cadence slows.
- Discord/Slack presence drops.

### Public hints

- Tweets about being overwhelmed.
- Blog posts about open source sustainability.
- Funding requests becoming more urgent.

## What to Do

When you sense maintainer burnout:

### Reduce load

- Open fewer issues.
- Submit fewer PRs.
- Make your PRs *easier* (smaller, well-described, ready).
- Don't ping unless necessary.

### Offer help

> "Saw the project's been busy. Happy to triage some issues if it'd
> help — would that be useful or get in the way?"

Some maintainers love this. Others find it intrusive. Offer; accept
their answer.

### Pick smaller wins

- A docs PR is welcome.
- A test PR is welcome.
- A 50-line clear fix is welcome.

A 1000-line refactor proposal is not welcome to someone burned out.

### Don't take silence personally

Burned-out maintainers aren't ignoring you specifically. They're
swamped.

### Consider forking with intention

If the project is genuinely stalled and you depend on it, consider:

- Maintaining a friendly fork.
- Coordinating with the original maintainer ("can I help you maintain,
  or fork?").
- Coordinating with other users to share burden.

A fork done with kindness, with offers to merge back, isn't hostile.

## Signs in Yourself

If you're (or becoming) a maintainer:

### Energy

- Dread when you see PR notifications.
- Putting off reviews you used to enjoy.
- Avoiding the project's chat channel.

### Behavior

- Becoming terse where you used to be warm.
- Closing issues without explanation.
- Snapping at contributors.
- "I'm fine" while clearly not.

### Time

- Spending more time on review than you can afford.
- Compromising sleep / family / health.
- Resentment of users who "don't appreciate" the work.

## Prevention

### Set expectations publicly

- Document response times ("I check this project weekly").
- Be explicit about scope ("we're not adding new features in 2026").
- Reduce surface area when needed.

### Recruit co-maintainers

The single biggest sustainability move. Even one co-maintainer halves
load.

Path:
1. Identify trusted contributors.
2. Invite gradually (reviewer first, then maintainer).
3. Document for newcomers.
4. Share decisions.

### Decline gracefully

It's OK to say:

- "Not accepting new feature PRs right now."
- "Limiting time on this project to 2h/week."
- "Closing all bugs without reproducers."

These aren't being a bad maintainer. They're being sustainable.

### Take breaks

- Public hiatus is fine. "I'll be back in October."
- Vacation responder helps.
- Hand off triage to a co-maintainer.

### Get paid

Not for everyone, but worth knowing:

- GitHub Sponsors.
- Open Collective.
- Tidelift / Polar.
- Foundation-hosted projects sometimes have funding.

Paid maintenance is more sustainable than unpaid.

## Public Discussion of Burnout

The OSS community has gotten better at discussing burnout publicly:

- High-profile cases (core-js, faker.js, ua-parser).
- Industry reports on sustainability.
- Funding initiatives.

If you maintain a popular project, knowing this landscape helps:

- You're not alone.
- There are resources.
- Asking for help is normal.

## What Burnout Looks Like in Big Projects

Even large projects with many maintainers experience burnout:

- A core maintainer announces sabbatical.
- A subproject loses its champion.
- A specific feature area stops getting reviews.

Effects ripple through the community. Patience and reduced demands
help.

## When the Project Has Already Burned Out

Sometimes you arrive at a project that's been burned-out for a while:

- Many open issues, no triage.
- Last commit 6 months ago.
- Maintainer absent from chat.

Options:

- **Use it as-is**, accepting no updates.
- **Fork** to maintain your own.
- **Find an alternative project.**

Don't pile on the dead maintainer with "please fix" requests.

## Recovery After Burnout

If you've burned out as a maintainer:

- Take real time off — weeks, not days.
- Don't read project channels during.
- Come back with reduced scope.
- It's OK to step down permanently.

Some former maintainers find joy returning as contributors. Others
move on entirely. Both are valid.
